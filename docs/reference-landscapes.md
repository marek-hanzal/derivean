# Reference landscapes

What real ground looks like, in numbers. The simulated world, the scouting tools and screenshot comparisons are measured against this catalogue. How the world is produced is described in [The simulated world](simulation.md).

## How to read an entry

- **Where** links to photographs on Wikimedia Commons (single files, or a search grid marked *grid*). Look at them next to the capture being judged.
- **Dimensions** are real-world ranges that can be checked in a height field or a screenshot with a known scale. A number with a link comes from that page. A number marked † is a specific figure quoted from the literature without re-checking it while writing; confirm it before turning it into a hard test threshold. Unmarked ranges are standard textbook ranges.
- **What you see** describes the ground with no plants. Earth photographs of wooded country were read as landform only.
- **Why it exists** ends with **Needs**: what a simulation has to contain for the type to arise.
- **Search recipe** is a set of measurable criteria for a scouting tool. Slope and relief thresholds assume a height field of 30 m or finer unless stated; "relief per 5 km" is the elevation range inside a 5 km window.

### A world without plants

Vegetation hides most of what Earth photographs of temperate country would show. Bare analogues: alpine zones above the treeline, the Himalayan rain shadow (Spiti, Ladakh), Iceland's interior, Svalbard, deserts, the Antarctic dry valleys. Compared with vegetated Earth, expect:

- Bare regolith and rock everywhere: colour and texture come from rock type, grain size and wetness only.
- Much denser fine dissection: rills and gullies on any weak material with a slope; drainage density several times to a hundred times higher (see [Earth as a whole](#earth-as-a-whole)).
- Rivers with sand or gravel beds braid; single-thread meanders need cohesive mud banks or rock confinement.
- Wind moves sand freely away from beaches, river beds and dry lake floors: sand sheets and dunes far outside deserts.
- Scree, block fields and fans stay sharp and unsoftened; slope foot angles are crisp.
- No peat, no marsh, no organic soil: wet flats are bare mud, silt or salt.

### How types sit together

Types change every 5 to 50 km along a real traverse, and the transitions are edges, not blends.

| Traverse | Distance | Country passed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Elbe lowland to the Krkonoše crest (Czechia) | ~70 km | loess and river-terrace lowland 200 m, sandstone plateaus with rock cities and cuestas 300–500 m, isolated volcanic cones, rolling crystalline upland 500–900 m, summit plateau 1 300–1 600 m with cirques cut into it |
| Bern to the Rhône valley (Switzerland) | ~90 km | till and molasse hill country 500–900 m, limestone Prealps with cliffs 1 500–2 200 m, glaciated high range 3 500–4 300 m, a 2–4 km wide flat trough floor at 500–650 m |
| Owens Valley across Death Valley (USA) | ~150 km | fault-front range 4 400 m, basin floor 1 100 m with fans and a dry lake, desert range 3 300 m, salt pan at −86 m, bajadas, dunes |
| Sognefjord mouth to the ice cap (Norway) | ~200 km | skerries and low rock coast, fjord walls 1 000–1 500 m, rolling high plateau with lakes and block fields, outlet glaciers |

## A. Young high mountains

### A1. Collisional high range

- **Where:** Bernese Alps and Aletsch Glacier ([grid](https://commons.wikimedia.org/w/index.php?search=Aletsch+Glacier+Bernese+Alps+aerial&title=Special:MediaSearch&type=image)); Karakoram, [Baltoro Glacier from the air](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Baltoro_glacier_from_air.jpg), [Concordia](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Concordia_%28Karakoram%29.jpg); also Mont Blanc massif, Himalaya, Southern Alps of New Zealand.
- **Dimensions:**
  - Range 100–350 km wide, 1 000–2 500 km long; massifs 10–40 km across.
  - Valley floor to summit 1 500–3 000 m (Alps), 3 000–5 000 m (Karakoram, Himalaya); relief per 5 km 1 000–2 500 m. Western Southern Alps: local relief averages 0.65–0.68 km, exceeds 1.5 km in the core ([Korup et al. 2005](https://people.wou.edu/~taylors/g407/korup_etal_2005_SHIVERS.pdf)).
  - Main valleys 10–30 km apart, side valleys 2–5 km apart; ridge crest to valley axis 2–6 km.
  - Slopes: mean 25–32°, most frequent 33–40°; rock faces 50–80° sustained over 300–1 500 m; scree aprons 32–38°.
  - Valley glaciers 0.5–3 km wide, 5–60 km long; medial moraine stripes 20–200 m wide.
- **What you see:**
  1. Massive shoulders carrying small sharp teeth: summits are a minor part of the volume; the bulk is broad buttressed mass.
  2. Serrated skyline of horns and arêtes; no rounded top anywhere above the glacial trimline.
  3. Thirds: ice and bare rock on top; rock walls scored by couloirs every 50–300 m in the middle; debris below (scree cones, moraine, fans).
  4. U-shaped troughs with flat floors; sharp breaks at the trough shoulder and at cirque lips.
  5. Glaciers striped lengthwise with dark moraine bands; lower tongues grey with debris; milky braided streams leave them.
  6. Rock units change over kilometres: pale spires of granite beside dark pyramids of schist, banded limestone walls.
  7. Underfoot: angular blocks 0.1–2 m, gravel, and near the ice polished, scratched bedrock.
- **Why it exists:** Colliding continents thicken the crust and push rock up at 0.5–5 mm/yr; rivers and glaciers cut down as fast, so slopes steepen until they fail (rockfall, slides) and hold a threshold angle. Ice widened and deepened the valleys; frost shatters the walls above it. **Needs:** uplift concentrated by structure, rock strong enough to stand as walls, threshold slope failure that produces scree, glacial erosion (troughs, cirques, overdeepening), rock units of contrasting strength. Without wall failure and debris the result is smooth domes.
- **Search recipe:** mean elevation > 2 000 m; relief > 1 200 m per 5 km; ≥ 30 % of cells steeper than 35°; ice present or formerly present; flat valley floor < 20 % of area; crystalline or massive carbonate rock at the surface; rock uplift > 0.5 mm/yr.

### A2. Glacial trough: the wide valley under a high range

- **Where:** Rhône valley in Valais, [view along the floor](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Rhone_Valley,_Valais,_Switzerland_-_August_2020_-_by_FloX04.jpg), [second view](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Wallis_Rh%C3%B4netal_1.JPG); Lauterbrunnen, [photochrom c. 1900](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Lauterbrunnen_Valley_with_Staubbach,_Bernese_Oberland,_Switzerland-LCCN2001701212.jpg); Yosemite Valley, [winter from Tunnel View](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Winter_Morning_Sunrise_Tunnel_View_Yosemite_Valley_-_Yosemite_National_Park_%285763418254%29.jpg); also the Inn valley.
- **Dimensions:**
  - Floor width: Lauterbrunnen under 1 km with walls up to 1 000 m, Staubbach Falls 297 m ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lauterbrunnen_Valley)); Yosemite 12 km long, 1.6 km wide on average, 910–1 070 m deep, with up to 610 m of sediment under the floor ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yosemite_Valley)); Rhône and Inn floors 1.5–4 km wide† for 50–150 km.
  - Floor gradient 2–15 m/km (0.1–1°); cross-slope of the floor under 1°.
  - Walls 60–90° in massive rock, 35–45° in weak rock; summits 2 000–3 500 m above the floor within 5–15 km.
  - Tributary valleys enter 150–700 m above the floor (hanging valleys).
  - Talus cones at the wall foot 50–300 m high at 32–38°; side fans 0.5–3 km radius at 2–8°; shoulder benches above the wall 200–1 000 m wide.
- **What you see:**
  1. A dead-flat floor meeting the wall at a sharp angle, softened only by talus cones.
  2. Parabolic cross-section; the valley runs straight or gently curved for tens of kilometres, spurs cut off flush.
  3. Waterfalls dropping free from side valleys that end in mid-air.
  4. A river wandering or braiding on the floor, pushed aside by fans from side valleys.
  5. Above the wall a bench of gentler ground, then the high peaks set back from the valley.
  6. Lakes where the floor was overdeepened or dammed by a fan, a moraine or a rockslide.
  7. Underfoot: gravel, sand and silt — the easiest ground in the whole range.
- **Why it exists:** Ice streams hundreds of metres to over a kilometre thick erode bed and walls; trunk glaciers cut deeper than tributaries. After the ice leaves, the overdeepened rock trough fills with lake and river sediment to a flat surface. **Needs:** glacial erosion that scales with ice discharge, overdeepening, post-glacial filling to a level floor, fans and talus. Without these every valley is a V with no floor.
- **Search recipe:** floor with slope < 2° at least 800 m wide and 10 km long; on both sides slopes > 40° rising ≥ 300 m within 1 km of the floor edge; summits ≥ 1 500 m above the floor within 10 km; alluvium or lake sediment ≥ 50 m thick; tributary mouths ≥ 150 m above the floor.

### A3. Cirques, arêtes, horns, tarns and alpine lakes

- **Where:** [Oeschinensee](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Oeschinensee_0437.jpg) under the Blüemlisalp; Kotel cirques, Krkonoše, [in winter](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Kotel,_zima..JPG); also Tatra lakes, Matterhorn, Lake Louise.
- **Dimensions:**
  - Cirque 0.4–1.5 km wide and about as long; headwall 150–600 m high (over 1 000 m in the highest ranges) at 45–70°; floor under 10–15°, often tilted back into the mountain.
  - Tarn 100–800 m across, 5–50 m deep; lip is a rock bar or a moraine 5–50 m high.
  - Arête crest 1–20 m wide, flanks 45–70°, 200–800 m of relief; horn faces 500–1 200 m.
  - Cirques 0.8–2 km apart along a ridge, mostly on the shaded, leeward side.
- **What you see:**
  1. An armchair hollow: steep rock back, flat or basin floor, a lip, then a drop.
  2. A lake in the basin, turquoise or dark, leaving over the lip as a cascade.
  3. Scree fans from headwall gullies running into the lake; late snow at the wall foot.
  4. Ridges between neighbouring cirques narrowed to a jagged blade of stacked blocks.
  5. Where three or four cirques meet, a pyramid peak with distinct faces and edges.
  6. In old mountains the cirque is a sharp-edged bowl bitten into a smooth plateau — the strongest contrast in such country.
- **Why it exists:** A small glacier rotates in a hollow, plucks the headwall and grinds the floor; frost attacks the wall above the ice. Cirques growing from opposite sides sharpen divides. **Needs:** snow balance by elevation and aspect, local ice flow with erosion under sliding ice, headwall retreat by frost, lakes in overdeepened basins.
- **Search recipe:** basin 0.3–1.5 km across near the present or former snowline, enclosed over ≥ 180° by slopes > 40° at least 150 m high; floor slope < 12°; standing water or flat fill; aspect poleward or leeward.

### A4. Fault-block range front

- **Where:** Teton Range, [from Jackson Hole](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Barns_grand_tetons.jpg), [Snake River view](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Adams_The_Tetons_and_the_Snake_River.jpg); Sierra Nevada east face over Owens Valley, [escarpment](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:SierraEscarpmentCA.jpg), [with reservoir](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tinemaha_Reservoir.jpg); also Basin and Range, Wasatch, Krušné hory south-east scarp.
- **Dimensions:**
  - Front relief 1 000–3 300 m gained in 5–15 km: mean front slope 12–30°. Tetons about 2 100 m above the basin floor†, Sierra at Lone Pine about 3 300 m†.
  - Front straight or gently curved for 50–600 km; basins 10–40 km wide; ranges 25–35 km apart in the Basin and Range.
  - Triangular facets between canyons 200–800 m high at 25–40°; canyon mouths every 1–5 km.
  - Fans 1–10 km radius at 2–8°, merged into an apron; fresh fault scarps 1–10 m high across fans.
  - Back slope of the tilted block 2–10° and tens of kilometres long.
- **What you see:**
  1. A mountain wall rising from a flat basin with no foothills at all.
  2. A nearly straight base line; highest peaks right at the front.
  3. Faceted spur ends, like a row of flatirons, between V- or U-shaped canyon mouths.
  4. A fan at every canyon mouth; fans merged into a continuous ramp.
  5. Strong asymmetry: steep on one side, a long gentle rise on the other.
  6. Basin floor flat: a river belt, lakes behind moraine loops, or a dry lake bed.
- **Why it exists:** The crust is stretched; a normal fault slipping 0.1–2 mm/yr lifts one block and drops the other, and the basin fills with kilometres of sediment. The front is young, so erosion has not worn it back. **Needs:** normal faults with real displacement, tilt of the lifted block, basin subsidence and fill, fan building.
- **Search recipe:** linear break ≥ 30 km long where mean slope jumps from < 3° to > 20° and holds for ≥ 1 000 m of rise within 8 km; basin sediment ≥ 500 m thick beside it; opposite flank mean slope less than a third of the front.

## B. Old mountains and crystalline uplands

### B1. Old worn mountains

- **Where:** Krkonoše, [winter ridge](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Krkono%C5%A1sk%C3%A1_zima.jpg); Cairngorms, [plateau in winter](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Cairngorm_Plateau_Winter_-_geograph.org.uk_-_1392831.jpg), [Lairig Ghru trough](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Looking_across_the_Lairig_Ghru_%28geograph_4587204%29.jpg); also Jizerské hory, Krušné hory, Appalachians, Scottish Highlands, Urals.
- **Dimensions:**
  - Summit level 800–1 600 m; summit plateau remnants 1–10 km wide with slopes under 5–8°.
  - Valley to summit 300–900 m; relief per 5 km 200–600 m.
  - Valley sides 15–30°, mean slope of the range 8–18°; cliffs only in cirques, glacial troughs and scattered crags (100–300 m in cirques).
  - Valleys 2–5 km apart; loose mantle 1–10 m thick; bedrock exposed on under 5 % of the area.
- **What you see:**
  1. Long level or gently arched skylines; tops of the same height for tens of kilometres.
  2. Rounded convex summits passing into concave footslopes; a real summit dome (Sněžka) is a broad cone of rubble.
  3. Valleys V-shaped but smooth-sided; heads of valleys are shallow bowls.
  4. A few sharp bites: cirques and troughs with cliffs, cut into the smooth surface.
  5. Bare, it is stony tundra: frost-shattered blocks, coarse granite sand, lobes of creeping debris with 1–3 m fronts, stone stripes.
  6. Tors and block fields on crests; streams in bouldery beds.
- **Why it exists:** A mountain belt hundreds of millions of years old was worn to low relief, then raised 500–1 500 m as a broad swell or a fault block. Rivers cut back in from the edges; far from them the old surface survives. Frost and slow creep smoothed the slopes; only small glaciers formed. **Needs:** a long erosion history that produces a low-relief surface, later uplift, slow diffusion-like slope transport, relict surfaces surviving away from incising rivers.
- **Search recipe:** top 10 % of elevations within ±100 m over ≥ 20 km; mean slope 8–18°; < 3 % of cells steeper than 35°; relief 250–700 m per 5 km; crystalline or old folded rock; plateau-like top with incised margins.

### B2. Granite domes

- **Where:** Half Dome, [from Glacier Point](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Half_Dome_from_Glacier_Point,_Yosemite_NP_-_Diliff.jpg), [with the upper valley](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Half_Dome_with_Eastern_Yosemite_Valley.jpg); also Enchanted Rock, Sugarloaf (Rio de Janeiro), Stone Mountain.
- **Dimensions:**
  - Height above surroundings 100–600 m, diameter 0.3–3 km; Half Dome's sheer face about 600 m†.
  - Flanks convex, 30–70°, vertical where a slab has broken away.
  - Sheets 0.5–10 m thick parallel to the surface; vertical joints 10–100 m or more apart.
  - Weathering pits on top 0.2–3 m across.
- **What you see:**
  1. Smooth bare rock shaped like an upturned bowl; no peak, no ridges.
  2. Onion-skin slabs; curved overhangs where a sheet has dropped off.
  3. Few gullies because there are few joints; water runs down as sheets and leaves dark streaks.
  4. Pale grey to pink rock, uniform over the whole form.
  5. The base meets the ground abruptly; little talus, a few very large fallen slabs.
  6. In glaciated cases: polished surfaces and stray boulders perched on the dome.
- **Why it exists:** A body of granite with very few fractures is unroofed; release of load opens sheeting parallel to the surface. More fractured rock around it weathers and is carried away faster. **Needs:** a spatial field of fracture density controlling erodibility, sheeting parallel to the surface, low debris production.
- **Search recipe:** plutonic rock; joint spacing > 20 m; convex surface over ≥ 200 m; bare rock > 70 %; slope 30–70°; relief 100–600 m within 1.5 km.

### B3. Tors and boulder-strewn granite upland

- **Where:** Dartmoor, [Saddle Tor to Haytor](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Saddle_tor_to_Hey_tor_pano.jpg), [Fox Tor](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Fox_Tor.jpg); also Jizerské hory, Žďárské vrchy, Novohradské hory, Cairngorm summit tors.
- **Dimensions:**
  - Tor 3–20 m high (rarely 30 m), 5–50 m across at the base.
  - Blocks 0.5–3 m thick, bounded by joints 0.5–3 m apart.
  - One tor per 0.5–3 km of crest; fallen-boulder spread 50–300 m downslope on 5–15°.
  - Surrounding slopes 3–12°; relief of the upland 50–200 m per km.
- **What you see:**
  1. A castle or stack of rounded rectangular blocks on a hill crest or spur end.
  2. Horizontal joints dominate: blocks lie like stacked cushions; edges rounded.
  3. Perched and balanced blocks; narrow slots between stacks.
  4. An apron of loose boulders below; the hill itself is a smooth swell.
  5. Coarse quartz-and-feldspar sand underfoot; shallow rock basins on the top blocks.
- **Why it exists:** Rot along joints turns the rock around less-fractured cores into sand; later stripping of the sand (creep on frozen ground, wash) leaves the cores standing. **Needs:** varying joint spacing, a weathering front, stripping of the loose mantle.
- **Search recipe:** granite or gneiss; convex hilltop or spur; surrounding slope < 12°; local joint spacing 1–3 m inside a more fractured field; loose mantle < 1 m at the crest.

## C. Layered rock: sandstone country, plateaus and canyons

### C1. Sandstone rock city

- **Where:** Adršpach-Teplice, [towers](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Adr%C5%A1pach-Teplice_Rocks.jpg), [more towers](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Rocks_in_Adr%C5%A1pach-Teplice.jpg), [flooded quarry lake](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Adr%C5%A1pa%C5%A1skoteplick%C3%A9_sk%C3%A1ly_02.JPG) ([grid](https://commons.wikimedia.org/w/index.php?search=Adr%C5%A1pach+sk%C3%A1ly&title=Special:MediaSearch&type=image)); Saxon Switzerland, [Bastei](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Elbsandsteingebirge_%28066%29_Bastei_%2821880988642%29.jpg); Bohemian Switzerland, [Pravčická brána](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Prav%C4%8Dick%C3%A1_br%C3%A1na_%28Prebischtor%29_-_by_Pudelek.jpg); [Zhangjiajie pillars](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Sandstone_spire_forest_Zhangjiajie_Hunan.jpg); [Meteora](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Meteora_Rocks_in_Kastraki_Kalambakas.JPG); also Prachovské skály.
- **Dimensions:**
  - Adršpach-Teplice: about 20 km², towers up to 90–100 m ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adr%C5%A1pach-Teplice_Rocks)); common towers 20–60 m high and 5–30 m across.
  - Clefts along joints 0.5–5 m wide, 10–60 m deep, straight for 50–500 m, in two sets roughly at right angles, 5–30 m apart.
  - Massive sandstone 60–120 m thick, dip under 5°; walls 80–90°; tower tops level within 10–20 m.
  - Pravčická brána: span 26.5 m†, opening 16 m high, arch at least 3 m thick†. Bastei stands 194 m above the Elbe†.
  - Zhangjiajie pillars 100–300 m high; Meteora towers up to 300–400 m†, rounded, in conglomerate.
- **What you see:**
  1. Towers and fins of one height: pieces of a single plateau, separated by straight vertical clefts on a grid.
  2. Rounded heads and domes on top; vertical walls below with horizontal ledges following the beds.
  3. Overhangs, honeycomb pitting and a hard crust on the faces; iron bands in yellow, orange and grey; black streaks.
  4. Cleft floors flat and filled with clean quartz sand; piles of fallen blocks with crawl spaces at wall feet.
  5. The interior is still plateau; the city is most broken at its edges, where towers stand free.
  6. Water sinks into sand, returns as springs at the base of the sandstone; small pools and a lake with sandy shores.
  7. Arches where a fin was undercut from both sides.
- **Why it exists:** A thick, massive, flat-lying quartz sandstone is cut by vertical joints. Water, frost and grain-by-grain crumbling widen the joints; the debris is sand and is easily washed out, so walls stay clean and vertical. A weaker bed below is undercut. **Needs:** bed thickness and strength, joint sets with a spacing, widening of joints, removal of sand, free faces stable to about 100 m, undercutting at the contact below.
- **Search recipe:** quartz sandstone ≥ 60 m thick at the surface; dip < 5°; two joint sets 5–30 m apart; a weak bed exposed 100–300 m below the plateau; humid to sub-humid; relief 80–300 m per km at the plateau margin; no thick cover.

### C2. Table mountains, mesas and buttes

- **Where:** [Lilienstein](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Lilienstein_Saxon_Switzerland.jpg), [from the plain](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Lilienstein_%28415_m%29_v_cel%C3%A9_sv%C3%A9_kr%C3%A1se.jpg); Monument Valley, [mesa and buttes](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Mesa_and_Buttes,_Monument_Valley,_AZ.jpg), [buttes](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Monument_Valley_Buttes_-_USA.jpg); also Königstein, Szczeliniec, tepuis (Roraima).
- **Dimensions:**
  - Lilienstein 415 m, about 300 m above the Elbe†; cliff band 40–70 m†; top 100–500 m across.
  - Monument Valley buttes 120–300 m high†; mesas 0.3–10 km wide; tepui walls 400–1 000 m.
  - Profile: vertical caprock cliff 30–150 m, then a straight ramp at 28–38° taking half to two thirds of the height.
  - Outliers 1–10 km apart and within 1–20 km of the main scarp; top relief under 20 m.
- **What you see:**
  1. A flat horizontal skyline cut off by a vertical cliff: a plain in the sky, no summit.
  2. Cliff over ramp over plain; the three parts meet at sharp angles.
  3. All tops in a district at one level, because they are one bed.
  4. Cliff split into pillars by joints; slabs leaning away; blocks scattered down the ramp, smaller with distance.
  5. Colour bands of the layers continue from one mesa to the next.
  6. The plain between them is flat and bare.
- **Why it exists:** A flat resistant bed (sandstone, limestone, basalt) lies on weak beds (shale, marl). Scarps retreat sideways by undercutting; widening valleys isolate remnants. **Needs:** bed-by-bed erosion with a cliff-forming threshold, sideways scarp retreat, slope form set by rock strength.
- **Search recipe:** resistant bed ≥ 20 m over weak bed ≥ 50 m; dip < 3°; isolated top 0.01–50 km² with relief < 20 m; ≥ 80 % of the rim steeper than 60° over ≥ 30 m; ramp 25–38° below.

### C3. Cuestas and hogbacks

- **Where:** Swabian Jura scarp, [Albtrauf at Gönningen](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:G%C3%B6nnigen_und_Albtrauf.jpg), [outlier hills](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Rechberg,_Stuifen_und_Albtrauf.jpg); [hogbacks in Colorado](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Hogback_ridges_in_Colorado.jpg), [in Utah](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Hogback_ridge_in_Utah.JPG); also Broumovské stěny, Cotswold scarp, Comb Ridge.
- **Dimensions:**
  - Cuesta: beds dip 1–10°; scarp 50–400 m high at 20–35° with a caprock cliff 10–60 m; back slope equals the dip and runs 2–30 km; successive scarps 5–50 km apart. The Swabian scarp is up to about 400 m high and about 200 km long†.
  - Hogback: beds dip 30–90°; ridge 30–300 m high, 100–500 m wide, both flanks 25–45°, continuous for 10–200 km; parallel ridges 0.2–2 km apart.
  - Gaps cut by rivers every 2–10 km; outliers 1–10 km in front of a cuesta scarp.
- **What you see:**
  1. Asymmetric ridge: a short steep face showing the cut edges of beds, a long gentle back that is one bedding surface.
  2. Scarp line wavy in plan: bays, headlands and detached conical outliers in front.
  3. Cliff or ledges at the top of the scarp, a concave apron below.
  4. Hogbacks: ruler-straight parallel ridges with tilted slabs leaning against the mountain front.
  5. Valleys along the weak beds between ridges; streams follow them, then cross a ridge in a narrow gap.
- **Why it exists:** Tilted beds of unequal strength are etched: weak beds become valleys, strong beds ridges; the dip decides the symmetry. **Needs:** a dipping layered rock record and bed-by-bed erosion.
- **Search recipe:** resistant bed dipping 2–10° (cuesta) or > 30° (hogback), exposed continuously ≥ 10 km; scarp slope more than three times the back slope (cuesta) or about equal (hogback); ridge line within 10° of the strike.

### C4. Canyon lands and stepped plateaus

- **Where:** Grand Canyon, [Powell Point](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Grand_Canyon_Powell_Point_Evening_Light_02_2013.jpg), [South Rim panorama](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Grand_Canyon_%28South_Rim%29_Panorama_-w.jpg); [Fish River Canyon](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Fish_River_Canyon_Namibia.jpg), [2018](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Fish_River_Canyon,_June_2018.jpg); also Canyonlands, Blyde River.
- **Dimensions:**
  - Grand Canyon 446 km long, up to 29 km wide, 1 857 m deep ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Canyon)). Fish River about 160 km long, up to 27 km wide, about 550 m deep†.
  - Cliff bands 30–170 m high separated by slopes at 25–35°; benches 0.5–3 km wide; inner gorge 300–400 m deep with 45–70° walls.
  - Side canyons every 1–5 km; rim plateau relief under 50 m per 10 km; width to depth 5–20.
- **What you see:**
  1. Flat plateau, then the ground ends at a sharp rim.
  2. A staircase: vertical cliff in hard beds, slope in soft beds, bench — repeated four to eight times.
  3. Each band at the same height on both sides and around every headland.
  4. Plan shape branching without end: side canyons, amphitheatres, isolated buttes inside the canyon.
  5. Colour by bed: red, buff, white, grey-green; debris cones on the slopes only partly hide them.
  6. The river is small for the canyon; rapids where side canyons dump debris fans.
  7. A wide upper canyon with a narrow meandering inner canyon cut into its floor (Fish River).
- **Why it exists:** A plateau of flat beds is raised 1–2 km in a dry climate; a river fed from outside cuts down. With little loose mantle the slopes are controlled by rock: each scarp retreats at its own rate. **Needs:** a wave of incision from falling base level, layered strength contrast, rock-controlled slopes, sideways scarp retreat.
- **Search recipe:** surface with slope < 3° at least 20 km wide in layered sedimentary rock; incised ≥ 300 m by a channel with a large catchment upstream; width to depth 5–20; ≥ 3 cliff-forming beds ≥ 20 m thick; precipitation less than half of potential evaporation.

### C5. V-gorge with a waterfall at its head

- **Where:** Grand Canyon of the Yellowstone, [Lower Falls from Artist Point](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Grand_canyon_of_Yellowstone_and_Yellowstone_fall_nn_edit1.jpg), [lower canyon](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Lower_Yellowstone_Canyon_04.jpg); also Gullfoss gorge, Dyje and Vltava rock valleys at smaller relief.
- **Dimensions:**
  - About 32–39 km long, 240–370 m deep, 0.45–1.2 km rim to rim†; Lower Falls 94 m†, Upper Falls 33 m†.
  - Walls 35–50° with ribs and pinnacles; gullies 20–100 m apart; river gradient 10–15 m/km†.
  - No floor: the river fills the bottom.
- **What you see:**
  1. A sharp V; the river touches both walls.
  2. Walls of crumbling rock in yellow, orange, pink and white (rock altered by hot water); spires between scree chutes.
  3. Scree runs straight into the river; no terraces.
  4. A waterfall at the upstream end where fresh hard rock crosses the river.
  5. Flat plateau at the same level on both rims.
- **Why it exists:** A river crossing a plateau cuts fast through weakened rock; a band of fresh rock holds a step (knickpoint) that retreats slowly. **Needs:** river incision with rock-strength contrast, a held knickpoint, threshold slopes in weak rock.
- **Search recipe:** channel slope > 1 % cut ≥ 200 m below a low-relief surface; width to depth 2–4; side slopes 35–50° on both sides; floor no wider than twice the channel; step ≥ 20 m at the upstream end on a rock contact.

### C6. Badlands

- **Where:** Badlands National Park, [banded ridges](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:MK00609_Badlands.jpg), [second view](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Badlands_National_Park_kz02.jpg); also Bardenas Reales, Zabriskie Point, Chinle badlands.
- **Dimensions:**
  - Local relief 10–150 m; slopes 35–50° and straight; base plain 1–5°.
  - Rills 0.5–5 m apart, gullies 5–50 m apart; channel length in the hundreds of km per km² ([USGS](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/effect-climate-drainage-density-and-streamflow)), 600–800 at the extreme (see [Earth as a whole](#earth-as-a-whole)).
  - Ridge crests 0.1–1 m wide; colour bands 0.3–5 m thick; surface lowering 1–25 mm/yr†.
- **What you see:**
  1. A miniature mountain range: the same forms from 100 m down to centimetres.
  2. Horizontal colour stripes (grey, buff, pink, rust, yellow) crossing every ridge at the same height.
  3. Knife-edge ridges; slopes scored by rills; cracked, crumbly clay surface.
  4. No scree: debris leaves as mud.
  5. An abrupt slope foot onto a smooth, gently tilted wash plain.
  6. Flat-topped remnants above; pillars with cap stones; holes and tunnels where water runs underground.
- **Why it exists:** Weak, tight, clay-rich rock; storms on bare ground; nearly all rain runs off. A drop in base level starts a gully network that eats into the upland. On a plant-free planet any weak fine-grained rock with relief dissects like this. **Needs:** erosion by overland flow at metre scale, weak rock, low infiltration, very high drainage density.
- **Search recipe:** weak clay-rich sedimentary rock at the surface; relief 20–150 m per km at the edge of an upland; 200–500 mm of rain in intense storms; channel length > 50 km per km²; slopes in two groups, 35–50° and < 5°.

## D. Karst

### D1. Karst plateau: pavement and dolines

- **Where:** Malham, [limestone pavement](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Limestone_Pavement,_Malham_-_geograph.org.uk_-_4658163.jpg), [above the cove](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Limestone_pavement_above_Malham_Cove_-_geograph.org.uk_-_6266769.jpg); [large doline, Plateau de Saint-Barnabé](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Grande_doline_Plateau_de_Saint_Barnabe_35-36mod.jpg); also the Burren, Dinaric plateaus, the plateau of the Moravian Karst.
- **Dimensions:**
  - Pavement: blocks 0.5–3 m wide, fissures 0.1–0.6 m wide and 0.5–3 m deep along the joints; solution flutes at centimetre to decimetre scale.
  - Dolines 10–1 000 m across (usually 20–200 m), 2–100 m deep (usually 5–30 m); sides 10–35°, or vertical when formed by collapse; 5–200 per km².
  - Surface channel length near zero; dry valleys; Malham Cove is a dry cliff about 80 m high and 300 m wide†.
- **What you see:**
  1. A plateau with no streams and no valleys.
  2. Closed bowls pocking the surface, from a few metres to hundreds of metres.
  3. Bare grey rock cut into a paved floor of blocks; edges sharp and fluted.
  4. Red clay only at the bottoms of fissures and bowls.
  5. Streams vanish into holes at the edge of the limestone and return as large springs at the foot of the plateau.
  6. Dry gorges and white-grey cliffs with horizontal bedding.
- **Why it exists:** Rain dissolves limestone; water goes underground along joints and widens them into conduits; the surface is lowered in bowls instead of valleys. Pavements appear where ice stripped the cover. **Needs:** solution proportional to runoff, underground drainage so that closed depressions are legitimate, solution guided by joints.
- **Search recipe:** pure limestone or dolomite ≥ 100 m thick at the surface; precipitation > 500 mm; closed depressions 20–300 m across at > 5 per km²; surface channel length < 0.5 km per km² in a humid climate; springs at the base contact.

### D2. Tower and cone karst

- **Where:** Li River at Yangshuo near Guilin, [towers over the river plain](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:1_li_jiang_guilin_yangshuo_2011.jpg); also Ha Long Bay, Phang Nga, the Stone Forest (Shilin).
- **Dimensions:**
  - Towers 30–300 m high, 100–500 m across at the base, walls 60–90°, 200–1 000 m apart on a flat plain.
  - Cone clusters: 100–500 m of relief with closed hollows between, repeating every 200–500 m.
  - Stone forest pinnacles 5–40 m high and 1–10 m apart; notches and caves at plain level 1–5 m high.
- **What you see:**
  1. Steep hills rising like islands from a dead-flat plain; no foothills, almost no scree.
  2. Rounded or conical tops; walls grey-white streaked black and orange.
  3. Caves, overhangs and hanging draperies of dripstone in the walls.
  4. A river wandering across the plain between the hills and cutting under their feet.
  5. Rank behind rank of similar silhouettes fading into haze.
- **Why it exists:** Very thick, pure, strong limestone in a hot wet climate for millions of years with slow uplift. Solution at the level of the plain cuts sideways; the hills are what is left. **Needs:** solution concentrated at the water table and under river plains, a long warm humid history.
- **Search recipe:** massive limestone ≥ 300 m thick; precipitation ≥ 1 200 mm and mean temperature ≥ 17 °C; low uplift; isolated hills with height to width 0.5–2 and walls > 60° on a plain with slope < 1°; 1–5 hills per km².

### D3. Poljes, karst gorges, shafts and cave mouths

- **Where:** [Imotsko polje](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Imotsko_polje_VP01.jpg), [Feneos polje flooded](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Feneos_polje-floods.jpg); Verdon, [gorge](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Verdon_Gorge_1.jpg), [panorama](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Gorges_du_Verdon_panorama.jpg); Macocha, [from the rim](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Propast_Macocha_01.jpg), [from the lower bridge](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Macocha_z_doln%C3%ADho_m%C5%AFstku.jpg).
- **Dimensions:**
  - Polje: floor 1–60 km long, 0.5–10 km wide, slope under 0.5°; borders 20–40° rising 100–1 000 m; seasonal lake 1–40 m deep.
  - Gorge (Verdon): about 25 km long, up to 700 m deep, 6–100 m wide at the river, 200–1 500 m rim to rim†; walls 70–90° for 100–400 m.
  - Shaft (Macocha): 138 m deep, mouth about 174 × 76 m†, pools at the bottom.
  - Cave mouths 2–30 m wide and 2–20 m high; the largest exceed 50 m.
- **What you see:**
  1. Polje: a perfectly flat floor bounded sharply by rocky slopes; a river that rises from a spring on one side and sinks on the other; a lake in the wet season; rocky knolls standing out of the flat.
  2. Gorge: a slot with white, grey and orange vertical walls and a green river; side valleys dry or hanging; cave openings and great alcoves in the walls.
  3. Shaft: a vertical-walled pit open to the sky with a scree cone and a pool at the bottom and passages leading off.
  4. Cave mouth: a dark arch at a cliff foot, often with a stream coming out or a dry bouldery floor going in.
- **Why it exists:** A basin floored at the water table is planed flat by solution (polje). A river fed from outside keeps its course through rising limestone; with no runoff on the valley sides the walls stay vertical (gorge). A cave roof falls in (shaft). **Needs:** underground drainage with sinks and springs, lakes when inflow exceeds what the sinks take, conduits large enough to collapse.
- **Search recipe:** polje — closed basin ≥ 1 km² in limestone with floor relief < 5 m, inflow and no surface outlet; gorge — width to depth < 1.5, walls > 60° for ≥ 100 m, river sourced outside the limestone; shaft — closed depression with depth more than half its diameter.

## E. Volcanic country

### E1. Stratovolcano

- **Where:** Mount Fuji, [aerial](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Aerial_view_of_Mount_Fuji_20190324.jpg) ([grid](https://commons.wikimedia.org/w/index.php?search=Mount+Fuji+aerial&title=Special:MediaSearch&type=image)); also Mayon, Cotopaxi, Rainier.
- **Dimensions:**
  - Cone 1 000–3 000 m above its base, base 10–50 km across; Fuji 3 776 m.
  - Slope 30–35° near the summit, 10–20° mid-flank, 2–6° on the apron: concave upward.
  - Crater 0.2–1 km wide, 100–250 m deep; radial gullies 0.2–1 km apart, 10–200 m deep.
  - Side cones 50–200 m high; mudflow and debris fans reach 10–50 km.
- **What you see:**
  1. One isolated symmetric cone, far higher than anything around it.
  2. Slopes that steepen steadily upward; summit cut off by a crater.
  3. Radial ribs and gullies; loose red, black and grey cinder scree on the upper cone.
  4. Lava flows as tongues with side ridges and steep fronts 10–50 m high.
  5. Snow cap and radial glaciers on high cones.
  6. A smooth ring plain of fans; lakes at the foot where lava dammed valleys.
- **Why it exists:** Sticky magma above a subduction zone erupts lava and ash in turn and piles them at the angle of rest within tens of thousands of years, faster than erosion removes them; collapses and mudflows build the apron. **Needs:** construction from a point source at the angle of rest with a distal fan, placed by arc magmatism, then erosion according to age.
- **Search recipe:** isolated peak, radially symmetric (distance from the centre explains > 90 % of elevation variance); relief ≥ 1 000 m; slope rising with height from < 6° to > 28°; volcanic rock; near a convergent plate boundary.

### E2. Shield volcano and lava field

- **Where:** [Mauna Loa](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Mauna_Loa_Volcano.jpg); Iceland, [Ögmundarhraun](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:View_of_%C3%96gmundarhraun_lava_field_in_Iceland_from_Road_427.jpg), [Dimmuborgir](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Dimmuborgir_lava_field,_Iceland,_20240716_1314_1462.jpg); also Skjaldbreiður, Craters of the Moon.
- **Dimensions:**
  - Shield slopes 1–12°; Mauna Loa about 120 km long and 4 169 m high†; Icelandic shields 500–1 000 m high and 10–20 km wide.
  - Lava field 10–1 000 km² or more; surface roughness 1–10 m: rubble blocks 0.1–1 m, flow fronts 2–20 m, swellings and pressure ridges 1–10 m high.
  - Lava tubes 2–15 m across with roof openings; cinder cones 20–200 m high at 25–33°, in rows 50–500 m apart along fissures.
- **What you see:**
  1. A horizon that is one long, barely tilted line.
  2. Black to dark brown bare rock; no loose mantle, no streams, no valleys — water sinks at once.
  3. Either smooth ropy plates or a chaos of sharp clinker.
  4. Flow edges as lobed low cliffs against older ground; islands of older ground left inside.
  5. Collapse pits, arches and cave entrances.
  6. Rows of red and black cinder cones; flat-topped steep-sided table mountains where lava erupted under ice.
- **Why it exists:** Runny basalt from fissures spreads far on low slopes; the rock is so permeable that rivers cannot form for tens of thousands of years. **Needs:** flows that fill existing relief downslope, permeability that suppresses drainage, weathering that depends on age.
- **Search recipe:** basalt lava younger than about 100 000 years at the surface; regional slope 1–8°; channel length < 0.2 km per km² in any climate; rough at metre scale, smooth at kilometre scale.

### E3. Caldera

- **Where:** Crater Lake, [aerial](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Crater_Lake_Oregon_aerial_with_sun_and_clouds.jpg), [second aerial](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Aerial_Crater_Lake.jpg); also Askja, Santorini, Ngorongoro.
- **Dimensions:**
  - Crater Lake: basin 8 × 10 km, lake 594 m deep, rim 150–600 m above the water†.
  - Small calderas 1–5 km, large ones 20–80 km with a low rim; inner walls 35–70°, outer flanks 5–15°.
  - Young cone or dome inside, 100–300 m high.
- **What you see:**
  1. A near-circular basin ringed by an inward-facing cliff, scalloped by slide scars.
  2. A deep intensely blue lake, or a flat floor.
  3. A young cone standing as an island.
  4. Layers of lava and ash exposed as stripes in the wall.
  5. Gentle outer slopes mantled with pale pumice, finely gullied.
  6. No river in or out, or a single notch.
- **Why it exists:** A great eruption empties the magma chamber; the roof drops along a ring fault; water, lava and sediment then fill the hole. **Needs:** collapse topography created by an event.
- **Search recipe:** closed depression 2–20 km across, circularity > 0.8; rim to floor ≥ 300 m; inner slopes > 30°, outer < 15°; volcanic rock; lake depth more than 3 % of its diameter.

### E4. Columnar basalt, plugs and necks

- **Where:** [Giant's Causeway](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Basalt_columns_on_the_Giant%27s_Causeway_-_geograph.org.uk_-_2392156.jpg) ([grid](https://commons.wikimedia.org/w/index.php?search=Giant%27s+Causeway+columns&title=Special:MediaSearch&type=image)); Panská skála, [organ pipes](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Pansk%C3%A1_sk%C3%A1la%286%29_edit.jpg), [from above](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Pansk%C3%A1_sk%C3%A1la_shora.jpg); České středohoří, [cones over the plain](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Panorama_%C4%8Cesk%C3%A9ho_st%C5%99edoho%C5%99%C3%AD_od_Br%C5%88an.jpg); also Devils Tower, Shiprock, Trosky.
- **Dimensions:**
  - Columns 0.2–0.6 m across (2–3 m at Devils Tower), 5–30 m tall per tier; five to seven sides, mostly six.
  - A flow 10–50 m thick has regular columns below and thin curved ones above.
  - Necks and plugs 100–600 m above their surroundings, 0.2–1.5 km across, flanks 25–45° with a rock crown; Devils Tower rises 265 m from its base†.
  - České středohoří: cones 150–450 m high, 1–5 km apart.
- **What you see:**
  1. Walls of parallel pillars like organ pipes: upright in flows, fanned or lying flat in plugs.
  2. Stepped pavements of polygon tops.
  3. Dark grey to black rock with rusty joint faces; scree of prism-shaped sticks.
  4. In the landscape: steep cones and knobs standing singly on a gentle plain, each with a rocky top and scree skirts.
  5. Some hills flat-topped where a lava sheet caps soft beds.
- **Why it exists:** Thick lava or a shallow intrusion cools slowly and evenly and cracks into columns at right angles to the cooling surface. The feeders are harder than the ash and sediment around them and are left standing after hundreds of metres of erosion. **Needs:** cooling joints as a rock fabric, hard intrusive bodies in a soft host, regional lowering of the surface.
- **Search recipe:** basalt or phonolite body ≥ 10 m thick (flow) or ≥ 100 m across (plug) in softer rock; isolated hill with relief 100–500 m, base < 2 km, slope 25–45°, rock at the top; at least three such hills within 20 km.

### E5. Geyser basins and spring terraces

- **Where:** Mammoth Hot Springs, [upper terraces](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Upper_Terraces_of_Mammoth_Hot_Springs.jpg), [Orange Spring Mound](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Orange_Spring_Mound_at_Mammoth_Hot_Springs.jpg) ([grid](https://commons.wikimedia.org/w/index.php?search=Mammoth+Hot+Springs+terraces&title=Special:MediaSearch&type=image)); also Pamukkale, Norris and Upper Geyser Basins, Haukadalur.
- **Dimensions:**
  - Terrace steps 0.05–3 m high; rimmed pools 0.1–10 m across; whole terrace complexes 0.5–3 km long with 50–160 m of relief†.
  - Deposition up to millimetres per day where water flows†.
  - Geyser cones 1–4 m high; crust aprons 10–300 m across at 1–10°; pools 1–100 m across.
  - A basin covers 1–10 km².
- **What you see:**
  1. Staircases of scalloped basins, each overflowing into the next.
  2. Fresh deposit white; iron stains orange and brown; old dry terraces grey and crumbling. (The vivid greens and oranges on Earth are microbes.)
  3. Flat pale crust with sharp-edged, deep blue pools.
  4. Cones and mounds with a vent on top; steam.
  5. Thin sheets of warm water spreading over the crust; dead-flat ground between.
- **Why it exists:** Groundwater circulates over shallow hot rock, rises along faults and drops silica (in volcanic rock) or lime (where it has passed through limestone) as it cools and loses gas. **Needs:** a heat source, a fault, groundwater, and mineral precipitation that builds ground upward.
- **Search recipe:** young volcanic or caldera setting with high heat flow; fault crossing; valley floor or slope foot where groundwater emerges; for lime terraces carbonate rock at depth.

## F. Glaciated lowlands and cold ground

### F1. Glaciated shield and lake country

- **Where:** Canadian Shield, [Sandy Bay, northern Saskatchewan](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Aerial_View_of_Sandy_Bay,_Northern_Saskatchewan.webp); also Finnish Lakeland, central Sweden.
- **Dimensions:**
  - Relief 20–150 m per 10 km, 10–60 m per km.
  - Water covers 10–30 % of the area; lakes from 0.01 to 1 000 km², 2–50 m deep; shorelines three to ten times longer than a circle of the same area.
  - Rock knobs 5–50 m high and 50–500 m long: up-ice side 5–15°, down-ice side 30–70°, all aligned.
  - Gravel ridges (eskers) 10–50 m high, 50–500 m wide, tens to hundreds of kilometres long; loose cover 0–5 m.
- **What you see:**
  1. A low even horizon in every direction.
  2. A mosaic of water and low rock ridges; lakes drawn out in one or two shared directions.
  3. Bare rounded whaleback outcrops, polished and scratched, in pink and grey.
  4. Stray boulders everywhere, some perched on bare rock.
  5. Rivers that are chains of lakes joined by short rapids and falls.
  6. Bouldery shores; small sand beaches at the ends of gravel ridges.
- **Why it exists:** Ancient hard basement of low relief was scraped by continental ice: the rotted mantle removed, joints plucked, basins dug. Ten thousand years have been too short for rivers to organise the drainage. **Needs:** erosion by a regional ice sheet, unorganised drainage with thousands of lakes, stray boulders.
- **Search recipe:** crystalline basement at the surface; formerly under an ice sheet; regional slope < 0.5°; relief 10–80 m per km²; lake fraction > 8 %; closed depressions at all sizes from 0.1 to 10 km; more than 20 % of channel length passes through lakes.

### F2. Fjords

- **Where:** Geirangerfjord, [panorama from Skageflå](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Panorama_of_Geirangerfjord_from_Skagefla.jpg), [from the water](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Geirangerfjord_LC0188.jpg); also Sognefjord, Milford Sound, Scoresby Sund.
- **Dimensions:**
  - Length 10–380 km: Sognefjord 205–226 km and 1 308 m deep with a sill at 100–200 m; Scoresby Sund 382 km ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fjord)).
  - Width 0.5–5 km; narrowest arms about 250 m†.
  - Walls 500–1 700 m above the water at 40–80°; trough relief from summit to fjord bed 1 500–2 500 m.
  - Waterfalls 100–800 m; deltas at the head 0.2–2 km long; plateau above at 1 000–1 700 m.
- **What you see:**
  1. An arm of the sea with water from wall to wall; cliffs enter the water directly, no shore.
  2. Straight reaches and sharp bends.
  3. U-shape, a shoulder, then a rolling high plateau with snow and ice caps.
  4. Waterfalls as white threads down the walls.
  5. Small deltas and fans at side valleys: the only flat land.
  6. Rockslide scars and scree cones running into dark, still water.
- **Why it exists:** Ice streams draining an ice cap follow old valleys and fractures on a raised continental edge and erode far below sea level; a shallow sill remains where the ice spread and thinned. **Needs:** glacial overdeepening below sea level, strong crystalline rock, a raised plateau edge near the sea, flooding by the sea.
- **Search recipe:** trough 0.5–5 km wide with its floor ≥ 200 m below sea level and open to the sea; walls > 35° rising ≥ 500 m above sea level on both sides; length more than ten times the width; plateau above the walls; sill at the seaward end.

### F3. Till plains, drumlins, eskers and kettles

- **Where:** drumlins, [aerial](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Drumlin_1774.jpg), [around Horicon Marsh, Wisconsin](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Drumlins_around_Horicon_Marsh_in_Wisconsin.jpg); also the lake districts of northern Germany and Poland, the Irish drumlin belt, the Midwest till plain.
- **Dimensions:**
  - Till plain: relief under 10–30 m per 10 km, slopes under 2–3°.
  - Drumlins 250–1 000 m long, 120–300 m wide, length to width 1.7–4.1 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drumlin)); 5–50 m high; hundreds to thousands in a field, 300–1 000 m apart.
  - End moraines 10–60 m high and 1–5 km wide in arcs tens to hundreds of kilometres long.
  - Kettles 10–1 000 m across and 2–40 m deep; outwash plains 0.1–0.5°; stray boulders up to several metres.
- **What you see:**
  1. Gently swelling ground with no organised valleys.
  2. Swarms of smooth egg-shaped hills all pointing the same way.
  3. Belts of hummocks with many closed hollows and round ponds, beside belts that are smooth and flat.
  4. A winding gravel ridge crossing the country regardless of slope.
  5. Boulders scattered on the surface; stony clay, sand and gravel underfoot.
  6. Small streams in broad shallow channels far too big for them.
- **Why it exists:** Debris laid down and shaped under and at the edge of a continental ice sheet; buried ice blocks melt into hollows; rivers under the ice leave gravel ridges. **Needs:** ice-sheet deposition with a flow direction, dead-ice relief, outwash in front of the ice.
- **Search recipe:** till or sand ≥ 5 m thick at the surface; formerly ice-covered lowland; regional relief < 50 m; elongated hills with aligned long axes; closed depressions 10–1 000 m across; channel length < 1 km per km².

### F4. Frost ground: block fields, patterned ground, scree

- **Where:** [stone rings, Svalbard](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Permafrost_stone-rings_hg.jpg); [block field, Femundsmarka](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Blockfield_in_Femundsmarka_national_park_-_Ida_Brennodden.png); scree in the Himalayan rain shadow, [Pin valley, Spiti](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Right_Bank_Pin_Mudh_Spiti_Himachal_Jun18_D72_7114.jpg), [Jankar Nala](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Jankar_Nala_Scree_Grass_Juniper_Oct20_D72_18207.jpg); [Taylor Valley, Antarctic dry valleys](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Taylor_Valley_of_the_McMurdo_Dry_Valleys.jpg).
- **Dimensions:**
  - Block field: angular blocks 0.2–2 m, 0.5–3 m thick, on slopes under 10–15°, over 0.1 to tens of km²; block streams 20–200 m wide on 2–15°.
  - Sorted circles 0.5–3 m across (rarely over 10 m) with stone rims 0.2–0.5 m high; stripes 0.3–3 m apart on 3–15°; frost-crack polygons 5–40 m across with troughs 0.2–1 m deep.
  - Creeping lobes: fronts 0.3–3 m high, treads 5–50 m, on 5–20°.
  - Scree: straight at 32–38°, 50–800 m long, stones 0.02–2 m with the largest at the bottom; the "steep slope" population in Alpine lidar peaks at 30–36° ([Loye et al. 2009](https://nhess.copernicus.org/articles/9/1643/2009/nhess-9-1643-2009.pdf)).
  - Rock glaciers 200–1 500 m long, 20–60 m thick, front 35–40°.
- **What you see:**
  1. A surface of stones arranged into geometry: rings and nets on flats, stripes down slopes.
  2. Summits that are seas of angular blocks with no bedrock in sight.
  3. Under every cliff a smooth sheet of rubble at one constant angle; cones at gully mouths merged into aprons.
  4. Scree the colour of the cliff above it, in stripes.
  5. Tongues of debris with ridged surfaces creeping onto valley floors.
  6. Very few channels: meltwater seeps through the stones.
  7. In polar deserts: bare banded rock, glacier fronts ending as ice cliffs on dry gravel, frozen lakes.
- **Why it exists:** Freezing water splits rock into blocks, frost heave sorts stones from fines, thawed wet debris creeps, rockfall piles up at the angle of rest. This is the default look of high and cold ground on a plant-free planet. **Needs:** frost cracking that depends on temperature, debris with grain sizes, piling at the angle of rest, slow lobed creep; ring and stripe patterns are material-scale detail.
- **Search recipe:** mean annual temperature below 0 to −2 °C now or in the past; strong rock on slope < 12° gives block field; cliff ≥ 20 m above a slope gives scree at 33–37°; flat wet fine ground gives patterned ground.

## G. Rivers, lakes and lowlands

### G1. Braided gravel river and outwash plain

- **Where:** New Zealand, [braided river, Canterbury](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Braided_river._Canterbury_NZ._%2814663811351%29.jpg), [Mackenzie Basin](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Braided_River_In_South_Mackenzie_Basin.jpg), [Rangitata gorge](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Rangitata_gorge,_Canterbury,_New_Zealand.jpg); Iceland, [Skeiðarársandur](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Skei%C3%B0ar%C3%A1rsandur_%282024%29.jpg), [with the glacier behind](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Skaftafellsj%C3%B6kull_Hvannadalshnj%C3%BAkur_Skei%C3%B0ar%C3%A1rsandur_Panorama_01.jpg); also Alaska, Tagliamento.
- **Dimensions:**
  - Active bed 0.3–3 km wide in mountain valleys; outwash plain 10–40 km wide. Skeiðarársandur: 56 km of coast, 20–30 km from glacier to sea ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skei%C3%B0ar%C3%A1rsandur)).
  - Slope 2–10 m/km on gravel, 0.5–2 m/km on sand.
  - Single threads 5–200 m wide, 0.2–2 m deep; bars 50–1 000 m long with 0.5–2 m of relief; banks under 1 m.
  - Grain: cobbles 50–250 mm near the source, gravel 10–60 mm further down, sand at the far end.
  - Canterbury Plains: merged fans about 180 km long and up to 70 km wide at 0.3–0.5°†.
- **What you see:**
  1. A valley floor that is all river bed: grey gravel from wall to wall.
  2. Dozens of shallow threads dividing and rejoining around lozenge-shaped bars.
  3. Water milky turquoise or grey from rock flour, or clear blue.
  4. Bare bars of overlapping cobbles with sand in their lee.
  5. A sharp edge of the floor against the valley side or a terrace step.
  6. On outwash plains: a flat black or grey desert to the horizon, ice caps behind, round hollows left by buried ice.
- **Why it exists:** Plenty of coarse bed material, a steep slope and banks with no cohesion. On a plant-free planet this is the normal form of every sand- or gravel-bed river. **Needs:** bed-load transport with deposition, channel width that adjusts to discharge and bank strength, many threads on a rising bed, grain size falling downstream.
- **Search recipe:** channel slope 0.1–1 %; floor at least ten times wider than a single channel would be; gravel bed; catchment rich in sediment (glaciated or steep); floor cross-relief < 3 m and no levees.

### G2. Lowland river: floodplain and terraces

- **Where:** [paired terraces](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Paired_River_Terraces_-_geograph.org.uk_-_344655.jpg), [terraces at Numata, Japan](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:River_terraces,_Numata,_Gunma,_Japan.jpg); wide-floored valley in open country, [northern Mongolia](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Landscape_near_Jargalant_in_northern_Mongolia_2018_02.jpg); also the Elbe lowland, Platte River, middle Lena.
- **Dimensions:**
  - Valley 1–30 km wide; floodplain 5–20 channel widths; channel slope 0.05–1 m/km.
  - Sand-bed channels 50–1 000 m wide and under 2 m deep where banks are loose.
  - Meanders where banks hold: wavelength 10–14 channel widths, bend radius 2–3 widths.
  - Levees 0.5–4 m high and 50–500 m wide; cut banks 1–5 m.
  - Terraces: two to six levels; steps 2–60 m high at 20–35°; treads 0.1–5 km wide.
- **What you see:**
  1. A broad flat floor set below the surrounding land.
  2. A staircase of flat benches along both sides, ending in straight steep steps.
  3. Channel wide, sandy and split where banks are loose; winding where banks are mud.
  4. Curved bars, cut-off bends as crescent lakes, swales of abandoned channels.
  5. Clean sand or gravel on the inside of bends, a vertical cut bank outside.
  6. Cracked mud sheets after floods; bluffs 10–80 m where the river undercuts the valley side.
- **Why it exists:** The river alternates between cutting down and filling as climate changes its sediment load, leaving old floors as terraces; the floodplain is built by sideways shifting and overbank mud. **Needs:** sideways channel migration, alternating incision and filling through climate cycles, overbank fines.
- **Search recipe:** floor slope < 0.1 %; floor ≥ 1 km wide; alluvium ≥ 5 m; flat surfaces (slope < 2°) ≥ 100 m wide parallel to the river at discrete heights 3–60 m above it, separated by steps > 15°.

### G3. Delta, coastal lowland and tidal flats

- **Where:** [Lena Delta from orbit](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Lena_River_Delta_-_Landsat_2000.jpg); Wadden Sea, [tidal channels](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:13-09-29-nordfriesisches-wattenmeer-RalfR-15.jpg), [Hallig Süderoog](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Aerial_image_of_the_Hallig_S%C3%BCderoog_at_high_tide.jpg), [banner](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Wadden_Sea_banner.jpg); also the Netherlands, Mississippi delta.
- **Dimensions:**
  - Delta plain 1 000–100 000 km², radius 50–200 km, slope 1–10 cm/km; tens to hundreds of branches 50–2 000 m wide; levees 0.5–4 m.
  - Small lake delta: top under 1°, front 20–35°, 10–500 m across.
  - Coastal lowland: relief under 5 m over tens of kilometres; about a quarter of the Netherlands lies below sea level†.
  - Tidal flats 2–20 km wide at 0.5–2 m/km; tidal range 1.5–4 m†; creeks 1–500 m wide, 100–1 000 m apart, branching.
  - Barrier islands 5–30 km long, 1–4 km wide; dunes 5–40 m.
- **What you see:**
  1. Land and water interlocked; a flat horizon.
  2. A river dividing into a fan of branches, each between low banks.
  3. Lakes, bays and abandoned loops between the branches.
  4. A brown plume spreading into green and blue water.
  5. At low tide: shining grey-brown mud and pale sand, creek systems branching like trees, ripples.
  6. A line of sand islands with dunes on the seaward side and flats behind.
  7. With no marsh plants the mud is bare up to the storm line, and blown sand moves inland.
- **Why it exists:** River sediment meets standing water and settles; rivers, waves and tides share the shaping; sinking ground and rising sea make room. **Needs:** deposition from the river plume with levees and channel switching, redistribution by waves and tides, a sea-level history.
- **Search recipe:** land within 10 m of sea or lake level; slope < 0.05 %; river mouth with a high sediment load; channels that divide downstream; mud and sand ≥ 20 m thick; for tidal flats a tidal range > 1.5 m and an intertidal zone > 1 km wide.

### G4. Lake with a sandy beach

- **Where:** Lake Michigan at Sleeping Bear, [point beach](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Sleeping_Bear_Point_Beach.jpg), [bluff and shore](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Lake_Michigan_Sleeping_Bear_Dunes.jpg), [gravel at the waterline](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Lake_Michigan,_near_Sleeping_Bear_dunes_%2820903362288%29.jpg); Adršpach, [lake in sandstone with a sand shore](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Adr%C5%A1pa%C5%A1skoteplick%C3%A9_sk%C3%A1ly_02.JPG); also Máchovo jezero, Lake Baikal (Olkhon), Lake Tahoe.
- **Dimensions:**
  - Beach 5–50 m wide (to 100 m on very large lakes); crest 0.3–2 m above the lake.
  - Slope at the waterline 3–10° in sand of 0.2–1 mm, 10–20° in gravel.
  - One to three underwater bars 20–100 m apart; pale shallows 20–300 m wide before the drop-off.
  - A formed sand beach needs 2–5 km or more of open water upwind.
  - Bay beaches 50–2 000 m long between headlands; old shorelines as ridges 20–60 m apart; dunes behind 2–30 m; spits 100 m to 10 km.
- **What you see:**
  1. A smooth arc of pale sand between darker headlands.
  2. Water grading from clear and pale over sand to dark blue at the drop-off.
  3. A wet band, swash lines and a small step of pebbles at the waterline.
  4. A low crest and a flat dry back beach; low dunes or a bluff of sand behind.
  5. A stream mouth turned aside by a small spit, with a pond behind it.
  6. The opposite, upwind shore rocky or bouldery, with no beach.
- **Why it exists:** Waves sort sand and carry it along the shore from a source — an eroding sandy bluff, a river delta, crumbling sandstone or granite sand — into bays on the downwind shore. **Needs:** wave energy from open-water distance and wind, sorting of shore sediment, transport along the shore, a sand source nearby.
- **Search recipe:** lake ≥ 1 km² with ≥ 2 km of open water facing the shore segment; nearshore slope 1–5°; sand source (sandstone, granite sand, outwash, delta) within 5 km up-drift; concave shoreline facing the prevailing wind; back-shore slope < 10°.

## H. Sea coasts

### H1. Cliffed coast: cliffs, stacks, arches, rock platforms

- **Where:** Étretat, [Falaise d'Amont](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Falaises_d%27Amont.jpg), [cliffs and bay](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:La_chapelle_Notre-Dame-de-la-Garde_et_les_falaises_d%27Etretat.jpg); Twelve Apostles, [from the east](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:The_Twelve_Apostles_Victoria_Australia_East.jpg), [stacks](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:The_Twelve_Apostles_4572.jpg); also Cliffs of Moher, Old Man of Hoy.
- **Dimensions:**
  - Cliffs commonly 10–200 m (Étretat 70–90 m†, Moher up to 214 m†); the highest sea cliffs reach 600–1 000 m; 75–90° in strong or well-bedded rock.
  - Retreat 0.01–2 m/yr; notch at the foot 1–3 m deep.
  - Rock platform 20–500 m wide at 0–3°, ending in a 1–3 m drop at low-water level.
  - Stacks 10–140 m high and 5–50 m across, within 20–300 m of the cliff (Twelve Apostles about 45 m†, Old Man of Hoy 137 m†); arches span 5–30 m.
  - Cove beaches of cobbles 50–500 m long at 8–20°; headlands 0.5–5 km apart.
- **What you see:**
  1. Level land cut off in a wall; the cliff shows the rock in section, in flat or tilted bands.
  2. At the foot a notch and fallen blocks, then a flat rock bench awash, then deep water.
  3. Stacks and arches in line off the headlands, with the same layers and the same top level as the mainland.
  4. Coves with steep cobble beaches between headlands.
  5. A white surf line; water colour changes at the edge of the bench.
- **Why it exists:** Waves undercut at sea level; the cliff falls and retreats, leaving a planed bench; joints and faults open into caves, then arches, then stacks. **Needs:** wave erosion at sea level with retreat depending on rock strength, a steady sea level, faster erosion along joints, removal of the fallen rock.
- **Search recipe:** ≥ 20 m of relief within 100 m of the shoreline; slope > 60° at the shoreline; open water ≥ 100 km to seaward; medium to strong rock; bench shallower than 2 m for 20–500 m offshore; isolated rock columns within 300 m.

### H2. Sandy coast: beaches, spits, barrier islands, coastal dunes

- **Where:** Curonian Spit, [aerial at Epha Dune](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Curonian_Spit_NP_05-2017_img17_aerial_view_at_Epha_Dune.jpg), [dune slope over the lagoon](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Dune_slope_with_view_over_the_lagoon_at_Curonian_Spit,_Lithuania.jpg); Wadden islands ([grid](https://commons.wikimedia.org/w/index.php?search=Wadden+Sea+aerial&title=Special:MediaSearch&type=image)); also Outer Banks, Dune du Pilat, Słowiński dunes, Chesil Beach.
- **Dimensions:**
  - Curonian Spit 98 km long, 0.4–3.8 km wide, dunes to about 60 m†.
  - Barrier islands 2–100 km long, 0.2–5 km wide, 1–10 m above the sea; lagoon behind 1–50 km wide; inlets every 5–50 km with fan-shaped shoals.
  - Sea beach 30–200 m wide; slope 1–5° in fine sand, 5–10° in coarse sand, 10–25° in gravel; crest 1–3 m; underwater bars 50–300 m apart.
  - Foredune 3–15 m; dune ridges 30–100 m apart; the Dune du Pilat is about 100–110 m high, 2.7 km long and 500 m wide†; slip faces 30–34°.
- **What you see:**
  1. A straight or gently curved pale shore for tens of kilometres.
  2. Bands parallel to the shore: bars and surf, beach, dune ridge, dune field, flats, lagoon.
  3. A narrow strip of land between two waters.
  4. Inlets with fans of shoals on both sides; hooked ridges at the ends of spits.
  5. A great bare dune moving inland, steep on the inland side.
  6. With no plants the sand does not stop at the foredune: sheets and dunes march inland.
- **Why it exists:** Much sand, waves, transport along the shore and a gently sloping sea floor during a slow rise of sea level; wind takes dry beach sand inland. **Needs:** transport along the shore, building of barriers and lagoons, wind transport from beaches.
- **Search recipe:** coastal plain slope < 0.2 % and sea floor slope < 0.1 %; sand supply from rivers or glacial deposits; wave-dominated (tidal range < 4 m); shoreline straight for ≥ 10 km; water body landward of a sand strip ≤ 5 km wide.

## I. Deserts

### I1. Sand seas and dunes

- **Where:** Namib at Sossusvlei, [dune and plain](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Sossusvlei_Dune_Namib_Desert_Namibia_Luca_Galuzzi_2004.JPG), [Dead Vlei pan](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Dead_Vlei,_Sossusvlei,_Namibia,_2018-08-06,_DD_041-049_PAN.jpg) ([grid](https://commons.wikimedia.org/w/index.php?search=Sossusvlei+dunes&title=Special:MediaSearch&type=image)); crescent dunes, [southern Brazil](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Barchan_Dunes_and_Lagoons,_Southern_Brazil.jpg); also Rub' al Khali, Grand Erg Oriental, Badain Jaran.
- **Dimensions** ([Wikipedia: Dune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dune) for the named figures):
  - Slip face 30–34°, windward side 5–15°; nothing steeper anywhere in the field.
  - Crescent dunes (barchans) 3–30 m high, 30–300 m wide, moving 5–100 m/yr and more.
  - Cross-wind ridges 100–1 000 m apart, up to 3 km in the largest.
  - Linear dunes up to 300 m high and 300 km long, 1.5–2.5 km apart.
  - Star dunes 100–500 m high and 1–3 km across; Sossusvlei dunes exceed 300 m.
  - Ripples 5–20 cm apart, under 3 cm high; sand 0.1–0.5 mm.
  - Flats between dunes 0.1–3 km wide; sand covers only about a fifth of desert area†.
- **What you see:**
  1. Repeating ridges with knife-edge crests, evenly spaced over tens of kilometres.
  2. Every dune lopsided: a long gentle ramp and a short steep face at one constant angle.
  3. Crest lines sinuous; sand tongues sliding on the steep faces.
  4. Yellow-white to orange-red sand; the older, the redder.
  5. Hard flat floors between dunes: white clay or salt pans, gravel.
  6. Ripples on every surface; no channels at all.
  7. A sharp edge where the sand sea meets rock desert.
- **Why it exists:** A sand supply (river plains, dry lakes, coasts, crumbling sandstone), wind above the threshold for moving sand, and deposition where the wind slackens. Wind regime and sand supply choose the form: one direction and little sand gives crescents, one direction and much sand gives cross-wind ridges, two directions give linear dunes, many give stars. **Needs:** wind transport of sand with a wind regime.
- **Search recipe:** precipitation under a fifth of potential evaporation; basin or lowland downwind of a sand source; strong winds; loose sand ≥ 5 m; relief 10–300 m with a slope histogram that spikes at 30–34° and ends there.

### I2. Stone desert, rock desert and island mountains

- **Where:** [Hoggar hamada](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Hoggar-Hammada.jpg), [hamada in Mali](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Hammada%28Mali%29.jpg); Spitzkoppe, [panorama](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Spitzkoppe_360_Panorama.jpg), [at sunrise](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Spitzkoppe_Sonnenaufgang.jpg); also Uluru, Tanezrouft, Gobi.
- **Dimensions:**
  - Plains under 1–2° for tens to hundreds of kilometres.
  - Stone pavement: stones 5–100 mm, one stone thick, covering 50–95 % of the surface, over 5–50 cm of stone-free silt.
  - Rock desert: bedrock plateau strewn with blocks 0.1–1 m.
  - Island mountains 50–700 m above the plain, sides 30–80°; the change from over 30° to under 5° happens within 10–50 m; 5–50 km apart. Uluru is 348 m high†; Spitzkoppe rises about 700 m above its plain†.
  - Rock-cut footslopes 1–6° and 0.5–5 km wide; wind-cut ridges 1–30 m high in parallel sets.
- **What you see:**
  1. An immense flat of dark stones fitted like a mosaic.
  2. Each stone coated dark brown to black and polished or faceted by wind; pale silt just under them.
  3. Bedrock ledges at knee height; no loose mantle to speak of.
  4. Solitary rock hills rising abruptly like islands: bare domes or piles of giant boulders, red and orange.
  5. A fringe of fallen boulders, then at once the flat.
  6. Dry wash lines marked only by paler sand and a little more relief.
- **Why it exists:** Under long dryness wind and wash remove fines and leave a coarse armour. Island mountains are little-fractured rock left behind as the surrounding surface is lowered and scarps retreat; sheet floods plane the footslopes. **Needs:** removal of fines leaving an armour, rock-controlled slopes with a sharp foot, resistant compartments, long time at low uplift.
- **Search recipe:** precipitation under a fifth of potential evaporation; slope < 2° over ≥ 10 km with a gravel lag on rock or old alluvium; island mountain — isolated hill with relief 50–700 m, mean side slope > 25°, break to < 5° within 50 m, granite or massive sandstone or conglomerate.

### I3. Range-front fans, dry valleys and salt flats

- **Where:** Death Valley, [fan from Dante's View](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Death_Valley_Alluvial_Fan_from_Dante%27s_View.JPG), [fan](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Alluvial_Fan.jpg); Badwater, [salt polygons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Badwater_salt_formations.jpg), [raised salt crust](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Uplift_on_the_salt_flats,_Badwater_Basin.jpg); also Bonneville, Salar de Uyuni, wadis of Sinai and the Negev.
- **Dimensions:**
  - Fans 0.5–20 km in radius (usually 1–10 km), 50–1 000 m from apex to toe; slope 5–10° at the apex falling to 1–3° at the toe; surface relief 0.5–3 m; boulders 1–3 m at the apex, sand at the toe.
  - Merged fan apron 2–15 km wide along the range.
  - Dry valley (wadi): flat sand or gravel floor 5–500 m wide, banks 1–10 m vertical in alluvium or rock walls 10–300 m; gradient 0.5–3 %.
  - Dry lake: 1–10 000 km², slope under 0.2 m/km; Uyuni is about 10 600 km² with under a metre of relief†.
  - Salt polygons 0.5–3 m across with rims 2–15 cm; mud cracks 5–50 cm.
  - Old shorelines as benches 2–20 m high on the basin sides.
- **What you see:**
  1. A low cone at every canyon mouth, spreading onto the basin.
  2. Fan surface streaked: pale active channels on dark, coated older ground; a trench at the fan head.
  3. Fans merged into a ramp leading to a dead-flat centre.
  4. Centre white with salt, tan with clay, or a mirror of shallow water.
  5. Honeycomb ridges on the salt; rings of sand, mud and salt around the centre.
  6. Level lines of old shores drawn along the hills.
  7. Dry valleys: empty flat-bottomed channels with steep banks, boulder bars and curled mud.
- **Why it exists:** Flash floods and debris flows leave narrow canyons, spread and drop their load. Basins with no outlet collect the water, which evaporates and leaves fines and salt; lakes rise and vanish with climate. **Needs:** closed-basin water balance with evaporation, grain size falling down the fan, fan building by channel switching, salt precipitation, a lake-level history.
- **Search recipe:** basin with no outlet; catchments of 1–100 km² ending on ground gentler than 8°; contours forming arcs around canyon mouths; basin floor slope < 0.02 % over ≥ 5 km with salt or clay; precipitation under a fifth of potential evaporation.

## J. Plains and rolling country

### J1. Loess hills and loess plateau

- **Where:** [loess gully](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Loess_gully_-_Mapillary_%28YiI7arAT9F5534lEJSjRda%29.jpg); also the Loess Plateau of China, the Palouse, Kaiserstuhl, south Moravian loess country.
- **Dimensions:**
  - Loess 5–30 m thick in Europe and North America, 50–300 m in China; grains 0.01–0.05 mm; no stones.
  - Flat remnants 1–100 km²; ridges and rounded hills with 50–200 m of relief.
  - Gullies 10–150 m deep with walls 60–90° in dry loess; channel length 3–8 km per km²†.
  - Rolling loess hills 3–15°, 0.3–1 km from crest to crest, 20–60 m high.
- **What you see:**
  1. Uniform pale yellow-buff material with no stones and only faint darker bands.
  2. Vertical faces with a columnar look; sunken tracks and pits.
  3. Flat or gently rolling tops abruptly cut by branching steep-walled gullies with amphitheatre heads.
  4. Slumped blocks at gully feet; very muddy streams.
  5. Smooth rounded hills, steeper on one side.
- **Why it exists:** Wind-blown silt from outwash plains and deserts drapes older relief. Dry, it stands vertically; wet, it collapses and washes away. **Needs:** dust deposition downwind of sources and a silt material that stands in vertical faces yet erodes easily.
- **Search recipe:** silt mantle ≥ 5 m; within 500 km downwind of outwash plains or desert basins; semi-arid to sub-humid; smooth upland (slope < 5°) with gully heads; channel length 3–10 km per km²; gully walls > 50°.

### J2. Open basins, footslopes and rock-crowned hills

- **Where:** Mongolia, [basin and hills near Jargalant](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Landscape_near_Jargalant_in_northern_Mongolia_2018_02.jpg), [Khövsgöl Province](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Landscape_of_Kh%C3%B6vsg%C3%B6l_Province.jpg), [steppe](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Mongolian_Steppes_%286228096139%29.jpg); also the Kazakh uplands, Patagonia, the High Plains.
- **Dimensions:**
  - Basins 5–50 km wide; hills 100–600 m high with straight slopes of 12–25° and rocky crests.
  - Footslopes 1–5°, 1–10 km long, concave.
  - Valley floors 0.5–5 km wide at 1–5 m/km; river terraces 2–10 m.
  - Gullies sparse and 1–5 m deep; channel length 1–3 km per km².
- **What you see:**
  1. Long smooth concave slopes sweeping from low rocky ridges down to wide flat-floored valleys.
  2. Ridges crowned with crags, tors and patches of scree; hill slopes all of similar steepness.
  3. Gravelly thin mantle underfoot; shallow washes that widen downslope.
  4. A small split or wandering river with gravel bars on the valley floor.
  5. Salt lakes in basins without outlet.
- **Why it exists:** A dry continental interior with little uplift and a very long history: slopes retreat and leave gently sloping rock surfaces under a thin sheet of wash. **Needs:** slope wash that produces concave footslopes, rock-controlled upper slopes, basins with no outlet.
- **Search recipe:** precipitation 20–50 % of potential evaporation; relief 100–600 m per 5 km; ≥ 60 % of the area gentler than 5° and a second slope group at 12–25°; concave profiles; low channel density; old crystalline or folded rock.

### J3. Rolling upland and hill country

- **Where:** north Bohemian lowland and uplands, [country below České středohoří](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Obec_Kozly_%28okres_Louny%29_-_panorama_%C4%8Cesk%C3%A9_st%C5%99edoho%C5%99%C3%AD.jpg); foreland of the Swabian Jura, [hills under the scarp](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Rechberg,_Stuifen_und_Albtrauf.jpg); also Českomoravská vrchovina, the Ardennes, the Piedmont of the eastern USA.
- **Dimensions:**
  - Elevation 200–800 m; relief 30–150 m per km, 100–300 m per 5 km.
  - Ridge to ridge 0.5–2 km; slopes mostly 2–12°, valley sides locally 15–25°; cliffs under 0.5 % of the area.
  - Profile: convex top (radius 100–500 m), straight middle, concave foot.
  - Valley floors 50–500 m wide and flat; stream gradient 3–20 m/km in headwaters, 1–3 m/km in main valleys.
  - Channel length 1–4 km per km² under vegetation; several times more on bare ground.
  - Main rivers in rock valleys 50–200 m deep with sides of 20–35° and outcrops.
- **What you see:**
  1. A sea of swells with tops at one level, but with a clear order: shallow dells, small V-valleys, flat-floored main valleys.
  2. Broad convex hilltops; here and there a rocky crest or ridge of harder rock 20–80 m above the general level.
  3. Main rivers in deep winding rock valleys; a sharp shoulder between gentle upland and steep valley side.
  4. Bare: gullied footslopes, stony crests, small fans at dell mouths, wide gravelly floors.
  5. Never the same for long: a harder bed, a fault line or a river crossing changes the grain every few kilometres.
- **Why it exists:** Long-worn old rock, slow uplift (10–100 m per million years), a humid temperate climate: creep rounds the hilltops and streams cut the valleys; cutting has worked back from the big rivers only along the main valleys. Without plants wash gains on creep, so dissection is finer and edges sharper. **Needs:** the right balance of slope smoothing and channel cutting to give ridges 0.5–2 km apart (not 5–10 km blobs), ridges on harder beds, incised main valleys.
- **Search recipe:** elevation 200–800 m; relief 100–300 m per 5 km; mean slope 3–8°; convex hilltops 0.5–2 km apart; channel length 1–4 km per km² or more; trunk valleys ≥ 50 m deep with sides > 20°.

## K. Small features a walker meets

Sizes are typical; every landscape above contains several of these, and a walk of one kilometre in any relief should pass at least one.

| Feature | Size | Look | Where |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Crag | 3–30 m high, 5–100 m long | jointed face 60–90° with ledges; fallen blocks 0.2–2 m spread one to two cliff heights downslope | valley shoulders, ridge crests, glacial steps; every 0.5–3 km along an incised valley |
